Swapping

Every quote is compared across every venue the chain has, and the best one wins. On Ethereum that is Uniswap v2, v3 and v4, Curve, Sushi, Lido, zAMM and Tacit; on Base, Uniswap v2, v3 and v4, Aerodrome and its Slipstream pools, and zAMM; on Robinhood, Uniswap v2, v3 and v4 and the Deepstate book. Our own Precision pools and this page’s orderbook are quoted on all three. One route, one signature.

A handful of outside aggregators are asked too — 0x, 1inch, ParaSwap, KyberSwap, Enso, Bebop, OKX. They are compared on what they can be held to, not on what they advertise: a quote less its slippage, against the same number for the chain. One only wins if it beats the chain by a margin wide enough to cover the extra gas, and if none of them answer, nothing changes. Tap the ▾ beside the rate to see every venue that replied and what each was worth.

An outside route is executed through a small adapter that measures what actually arrives and reverts if it is less than your minimum. The adapter is named in this page's own bytes, so a bad answer from a node cannot swap it for another one.

Slippage is the worst price you will accept — move past it and the trade reverts rather than filling badly. Price impact is how far your own size moves the price; the page warns as it grows and will not let a very bad one through quietly.

ETH and WETH are interchangeable here. The page wraps or unwraps whichever way the pool needs, inside the same transaction.

Sending

A plain transfer, or a SLOW one: time-locked, and reversible by you at any point until it matures. Useful for large amounts and for addresses you have not paid before. SLOW sits at one address on all three chains — Ethereum, Base and Robinhood — so the lock is offered wherever you are, and a position belongs to the chain it was made on.

From Ethereum a send can land on Base or Robinhood instead. It goes through that chain's own bridge — the same deposit the chain makes for itself — so no third party holds the ether in between; it arrives in a few minutes, and only ETH crosses. A time-locked one is routed through SlowArrival, which becomes the depositor on the far side and hands the reverse back to you: without it the bridge itself would be the sender there, and nobody could take the transfer back. Connect to the destination chain and a bridged lock of yours appears in this list with its Reverse button, alongside anything arriving for you. If a deposit could not be made on landing, the ether waits there under your name and this list offers it back.

The other way — leaving Base or Robinhood — has no canonical route that is not about a week, so the page opens a SlowRelay escrow instead. Your ether stays on the chain it started on; a relayer delivers the far leg out of their own inventory and is repaid from that escrow only against a canonical proof that they did. Nothing of yours enters a bridge, and there is no bond, oracle or committee. The recipient is paid in seconds either way — what waits is the relayer's own money, not yours or theirs.

Leaving an L2 is off until you turn it on, under “Another chain” on the Send tab. Not because it is unfinished, but because it needs someone on the other side: no relayer is obliged to take your escrow, and until one is running, opening one locks your own ether for eight days and six hours before it comes back. That is the whole of the downside — it cannot be spent while it waits, and it cannot be lost — and somebody has to go first for there to be anything to relay.

Two things to know before you open one. The fee is higher between the two L2s than it is to Ethereum, because that is the leg where the relayer waits about a week to be repaid rather than minutes, and a fee that does not cover the wait is one nobody fills. The escrow is remembered in this browser, because taking it back names every term it was opened with; if that record is gone the page looks the chain up for it, as far back as the node will answer. And if nobody does fill it, the escrow comes back to you after eight days — that floor is the contract's, and it is there so that a refund cannot beat a real delivery whose proof is still crossing. Until then it is yours but not spendable, so this is a way to send money and not a way to park it. If it is filled, the reverse is still yours on the far side for as long as the lock runs.

A locked transfer does not deliver itself — someone must send the claim once it matures. Tick auto-claim and the page posts a small tip so a keeper does that for the recipient. The tip is refundable: reverse the transfer and it comes back. Ethereum, Base and Robinhood all offer it, and each prices the tip from its own gas — on the L2s that includes the L1 data fee. Without it the recipient claims, which costs them almost nothing.

This panel does the common half. A guardian that has to cosign your withdrawals, balances held per token and delay, the full history in and out — those live on SLOW's own page, which SLOW serves from its own bytes the way this one does. The link under the delay opens it with what you have typed here already in it.

Orders

Fixed limit — a price you name. It rests until someone fills it or you cancel.

Dutch decay — starts above your floor and falls to it over a window you choose. It has two clocks: the decay, then how long it rests at the floor before it lapses; the escrow then waits until you reclaim it. Set that to Forever and it waits until you cancel.

Climbing bid — the mirror: a bid that rises toward a ceiling you name.

Orders are escrowed on chain when placed, so a fill cannot fail for want of funds, and cancelling returns the escrow. Boards are denominated in WETH — an order asking for ETH is asking for WETH, and pays out as such.

The book under the form is live and takeable. Yours sit at the top; everyone else’s come cheapest first for the pair above, each row carrying its own unit price. Fill takes a single order, in part where it allows it. That same book is priced into every Swap quote — a swap that beats the pools on resting orders says Orderbook beside the rate, with the share it took.

Liquidity

Precision pools are bands, not endless curves. A position earns fees only while the price is inside its band, and holds one asset or the other at the edges. A wider band is safer and earns less; a narrow one is the opposite.

The bar on each row shows where the price sits in that band. Out of range means it is earning nothing right now.

Add both sides, or tick one-sided and the page swaps half of what you put in on the way, in the same transaction; it reverts rather than mint less than it quoted. A pool that runs a hook cannot be quoted that way, so there you add both sides. Taking it back out takes all of a position, or a quarter, a half, three quarters, with the least you will accept priced on the part taken.

Where a farm is running, staking a position earns TAC on top of its fees, and either way round you can move all of it or a quarter, a half, three quarters. Taking the whole stake out claims the TAC with it; taking part leaves the TAC behind, and there is a Claim of its own for collecting it without unstaking.

Launching a coin

One transaction, and no ether needed aside from gas. A fixed supply is minted, your allocation is paid to you immediately, and everything else is seeded into a fresh ETH pool — one-sided, so the pool opens holding only your token.

Starting market cap is the pool's virtual ether reserve. It sets the opening price and how hard that price is to move, which are the same dial: roughly that much buying moves the market halfway. Small numbers mean the first buyer takes a large share of the supply, which is why the form says what one ether would buy.

Creator keeps is a share of the token SUPPLY, minted straight to you when the coin launches — tokens, not ether, and not a fee on trading. Keep 10% of a billion and 100,000,000 land in your wallet at once, unlocked, with nothing vesting. It is capped at 20%.

Whatever you keep does not go into the pool, and selling it later pulls ether back out of that pool — so a modest-looking percentage can be a claim on most of what early buyers put in. Anyone deciding whether to buy your coin can work that out too.

A logo is stored as contract code on Ethereum — no IPFS, no pinning service, nothing to go dark. Upload anything; the page compresses it and shows the size before you sign. You can replace it later.

Launching a cause

The other half of the launcher, and a different thing entirely: no pool, no market, no trading. Ten million units are offered at a fixed price in ether — your goal divided by ten million — and what backers pay accumulates in a treasury that belongs to the cause, not to you.

Two clocks, and they are deliberately different lengths. Runs for is how long backing stays open. Released over sets a fixed rate, not a fixed fraction: the tap pays out your goal divided by that window, every second, whatever was actually raised. Ten ether released over a year is under three hundredths of an ether a day.

So a cause that raises less than its goal empties faster than the window suggests. Raise a tenth of the goal and the treasury is drained in a tenth of the time — a tenth of a year, not a year. Since what has not been released is exactly what a backer can still burn back for, an under-subscribed cause can leave its backers with nothing well before the release window is over. Set the window long enough that a partial raise still leaves something behind, and read the daily figure below as the speed at full funding.

The release clock starts the moment you launch and runs whether or not anyone has backed you yet, which is why it wants to be much longer than the raise. Release over the same thirty days you raise for and the whole treasury is yours the day backing closes — and somebody who backed you on the last day gets nothing back, because there is nothing left unreleased.

That is what makes the token worth something on its own: whatever has not been released yet can be burned back for. A backer who changes their mind, or who watches a cause go quiet, redeems their share of the untouched treasury and takes their ether home. Nobody has to agree and nothing has to be voted on.

The creator can close that exit, but not quietly and not quickly. Burning calls ragequit, which the DAO can disable — and since the creator holds the only share, they alone can propose it and carry the vote. What they cannot do is act on it at once: the change has to go through the DAO, which queues it on chain and then waits out a three-day timelock before it can be executed. That queue is public from the moment it is entered, and burning back stays open the whole time. So the protection is not that nobody can take the exit away; it is that taking it away is visible, and slower than leaving.

Backers hold loot, which carries that claim and no vote. You keep a single share and the vote that comes with it, and you buy that share at the same price everyone else pays — so your claim on the treasury is the same shape as theirs.

Paste a cause’s token into the swap and the page prices the burn for you, against the treasury rather than against a market. It is the only price such a token has, and it is the honest one.

Fees

A launched market charges 1% per swap. Half of that stays in the pool as reserves, which raises the floor for every holder. The other half is collectable, and splits 80% creator, 10% protocol, 10% tithe.

The tithe is burned to Ethereum itself. The ether goes to the BETH burner, which destroys it and mints a BETH receipt recording that it happened. Nobody can spend it and it cannot be redeemed — the ether no longer exists. What it buys is a smaller ether supply, which accrues to everyone holding ether rather than to us. Base and Robinhood have no BETH, so there the tenth goes to the same address and no receipt is minted.

The token side of collected fees is burned outright too — a launched coin's supply only ever falls.

Names

A .wei name stands in for your address anywhere this page takes a recipient, alongside .eth and .gwei, and it is a token in its own right — it can rest on the order book like anything else. A name you own can also be made to carry your Tacit address, so that people can pay you privately by name.

Paid names start at 0.0005 ETH a year and take two transactions about a minute apart: a commitment, which holds the name for you for 24 hours, and then the registration itself. A .id.wei name is free and takes one.

roll.wei is a monthly draw among paid .wei names, free to enter. The odds are that name’s weight in the roll against every other entry; a free .id.wei name carries none, so only paid names go in. What is staked, how many are entered and how long the round has left are read off the chain under the form.

Private bridge

Tacit’s confidential pool, on Ethereum. Deposit shields funds from this wallet into a note — ether becomes tETH, and the other listed assets shield as themselves. Send privately pays another Tacit address, or a name that carries one: the amount and the recipient stay hidden on chain, and they have 90 days to claim it into notes of their own. Withdraw pays out to any 0x — ether on Ethereum, Base or Robinhood, everything else on Ethereum.

Your Tacit key comes from a single signature — the same key tacit.finance derives from this wallet, with a Bitcoin address of its own for cBTC. Nothing is kept for you anywhere: a restored key finds its own deposits on chain again, so there is no record to lose.

What is hidden, and what is not. A withdrawal’s amount, recipient and timing are public. What the chain does not show is which deposit it came from — so the privacy you get is the crowd you are in, and the other deposits in the pool are what make it. Withdrawing to the wallet you deposited from gives it away, which is why the page says so before you sign.

Settle decides who sends the transaction. Through the relay, it proves and pays its own gas for a fee; from this wallet, you pay no fee and send it yourself. The relay builds the proof either way, so it sees the link either way. An exit to Base or Robinhood needs one activate transaction on the far side — send that from a different wallet, or it ties the exit back to this one.

Markets

Parimutuel yes / no markets on Ethereum. There is no order book and no counterparty: every bet goes into one pot, and when the question is answered the winning side splits that pot in proportion to the shares it holds. So a payout is not fixed when you bet — the line under the amount is what your bet takes against the pot as it stands.

Every market names a resolver when it is made, and the resolver is trusted: they answer the question, they may end trading early where the market was made that way, and they take a fee, fixed at creation, out of the pot. If a side is empty when it resolves, or the resolver voids it, or nobody has answered thirty days after close, the market voids — every share, yes and no alike, takes back an equal part of the pot, and no fee is charged.

Two optional taxes, and both stay in the pot rather than going to anyone. Exit tax is what it costs to sell a share back before close; leave it at zero and bets are final. Late tax ramps from nothing at creation to its full size at close, so a later bet mints fewer shares and the earlier ones are worth more.

What this page is

There is no server. This page is served from a contract, executes only its own bytes, and needs nothing but a node to quote and swap. Those bytes cannot be changed by anyone, us included — a new version is a new address, not an edit. The token list is a contract too, artwork included; so are the node list, the solver roster, and the adapter an outside route settles through. It talks to your wallet, to Ethereum, and — when you ask for a quote — to the aggregators listed under Swapping. Which ones those are is itself recorded on chain and curated there, not decided by this page or by whoever answers a request; any of them can be switched off in a single transaction. They are asked for a price on a pair and a size, and they are not told who is asking: the address they are given is the shared adapter, the same one in every other person’s request. They never see a key, and they cannot move anything, because what they return is bounded on chain before it settles. Pin On-chain in the venue list to skip them entirely. WalletConnect is another: reaching your phone needs its relay. The private bridge has a relay of its own, and what it can see is set out under Private bridge. Reads go through a public node.

Your wallet’s keys never leave it. Nothing here custodies your funds; every trade settles from your address to a contract you can read. The one key this page holds is the Tacit key for the private bridge, and that is derived from a signature and kept in this browser — the same wallet derives it again anywhere else.

The token list is zList, ranked on chain by conviction staking. Anything not on it can still be traded by pasting its address.
